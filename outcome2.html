In today's world, AI is especially advanced. It can help you generate images or songs, understand what you say even with all the misspellings you made, solve math equations for you, or give you general knowledge about some topic. But, like everything else, it has its good and bad. We have already covered some of their good parts, but for the bad, it often revolves around ethical issues, such as bias and discrimination, misinformation, and impersonation.

In an article written by Janice Gassam Asare, "I Tested AI on Different Black Hairstyles. The Results Surprised Me," she talks about the experiement she did where she generate eight different images with different hairstyles and ask three AI tools, Clarifai, Amazon Rekognition, and Claude, to evaluate the professinalism. The ultimate outcome was that all three AI give the braid hairstyle as less professional and intelligence. AI does not intentionally discrimate against a certain groups. It doesn't have feeling or judgement of a human being to do so. However, the data that it learned might contain prejudice. Similar to a baby picking behaviors from their parents and surrounding, AI can also learn these training data and reintroduce the same pattern. As a result, if the AI was fed with prejudice data, they will become prejudice as well.

An unfortunate incident happened in 2024, as described in an article by the World Economic Forum, "‘This happens more frequently than people realize’: Arup chief on the lessons learned from a $25m deepfake crime." Arup, a UK engineering firm, lost $25 million after an employee was deceived during a video conference into transferring company money. The employee believed they were speaking to the managers, but the people who were in that call were actually deepfakes using the help of AI. AI is a very powerful tool, but it is also quite easy to access. Anyone can use it to help them do stuff, which also includes ill intent. AI ultimately is just a bunch of code combined together, and code doesn't inherently understand the intention behind every user's request; thus they can potentially be used to create convincing content for deception.

As powerful and smart AI has become , there is an undeniable fact, is that they can still make mistakes and gives out incorrect information. AI generates responses based on the patterns it picked up from its training data, or from a variety of websites. However, there is no guarantee that those sources it used are actually trustworthy or accurate. For example, in an article published by the University of Maryland, "Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Information Literacy: What does AI get wrong?" the authors demonstrate their mistakes by giving it a few prompts. These include generating a map of which African countries performed best in the 2026 World Cup, providing search materials about the Russian Revolution using the University of Maryland's library, making citations, or a summary of a World Cup match. The result AI gave revealed several of its flaws. For instance, the map it generated claims that Morocco got to the semifinal in the World Cup even though they didn't. The URL for the citation it gave didn't exist, and the links for the Russian Revolution search materials weren't correct, as they led to somewhere different. Also, the World Cup game summary that it claimed Argentina won against Norway was based on someone who played the game FIFA, rather than a real match. These happened because AIs were trained to give out information rather than evaluating the truth. They can provide responses that sound accurate and convincing when, in reality, they're false. They are just based on information they learn from their database and give responses. But when something is missing, or they can't find it, they may even generate fictional details to fill in those gaps.
